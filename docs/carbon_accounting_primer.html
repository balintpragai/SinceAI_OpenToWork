The point: for a food company, over 95 % of emissions sit on farms and in ingredients it does not own. The factories are a rounding error. The real work is estimating what happens on hundreds of farmers' fields and in dozens of suppliers' plants.

Vocabulary

Six concepts you will keep meeting

Location vs market-based Scope 2

Location-based uses the average grid. Market-based lets you claim green power by holding guarantees of origin (GoOs), certificates saying "this MWh came from wind". Loopholes: wrong vintage year, double counting, claiming more than you used.

Emission factor quality

The same purchase can be estimated three ways, from crude to precise:

1 Spend-based: € spent × kg CO2e per €
2 Average: kg bought × generic factor
3 Supplier PCF: the supplier's measured footprint

PCF (product carbon footprint)

A supplier's own footprint for one product, often in the PACT JSON format. Usually the best data, often broken: stale reference years, per-tonne values sold as per-kg, land-use change left out.

FLAG

Forest, Land and Agriculture: emissions from farming itself, such as fertiliser N2O, soil carbon loss and land-use change. SBTi requires food companies to set a separate FLAG target (3.03 %/yr).

Drained peat soils

Drained organic soil oxidises and emits about 29 tCO2 per hectare per year, against 1–3 t for a normal field. About 6 % of parcels can dominate the farm footprint, yet farmers often report "mineral soil" and the current calculator assumes it.

Base-year recalculation

Apetit sold its grain trade in 2022 and emissions "fell". That is not a reduction, so the base year is restated without it. Buying Foodhills (Nov 2025) works the other way: its emissions are added to the base.

Pain points

What sustainability teams actually struggle with

132,163 t vs ~6,400 t

The dataset's draft_report_summary_2025.csv shows a preliminary Scope 1 about 20 times the true value, and a Scope 3 of 71 million t. That is what an unchecked pipeline produces.

Data lives in a dozen places

SAP purchase lines, meter exports, fuel invoices, a technician's refrigerant log, waste contractor PDFs, supplier emails, 680 farmer surveys. A team of 2–5 people stitches it together in Excel for months each spring.

Errors are common and expensive

Kilograms read as tonnes, shifted decimals, euros mixed with kronor, duplicate invoices, missing meter months. One bad line in cat 1 moves the total by thousands of tonnes.

Not every oddity is an error

Credit notes, vessel cargo billed in tonnes, winter energy peaks and ammonia refrigerants are all legitimate. Naive rules over-flag them and waste expert time.

Auditors need a trail

Under CSRD the auditor asks where 172,085 comes from. The team must show rows, factors and every correction with its reason and approver. Spreadsheets cannot do this well.

Method changes look like reductions

Switching a supplier from spend-based to PCF data can cut reported emissions by 30 % with no tonne actually avoided. Separating real change from method, structure and data fixes is hard.

The rules keep moving

SBTi V2 and the GHG Protocol Land Sector standard arrive in 2027, the EU Deforestation Regulation in Dec 2026, ESRS keeps being revised. Someone has to read hundreds of pages each year.

Supplier and farm data improves slowly

You cannot chase everyone. The question is which few suppliers or farms have the biggest size × uncertainty, so better data there changes the total.

Numbers do not become decisions

"We emit 200,000 t" does not tell the CFO what to do on Monday.

The story we could tell

An AI-assisted carbon close: it catches errors, explains them, waits for an expert to approve, and every number is auditable down to the invoice line. A copilot then answers questions from those approved numbers.

The change bridge or the data-improvement list would be the differentiator on top.
